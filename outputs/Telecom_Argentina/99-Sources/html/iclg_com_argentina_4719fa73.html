1. Overview

1.1        Please briefly describe the: (a) telecoms, including internet; and (b) audio-visual media distribution sectors in your jurisdiction, in particular by reference to each sector’s: (i) annual revenue; and (ii) 3–5 most significant market participants.

The telecommunications sector, including internet services, is a significant economic contributor in Argentina.  In 2020, the top four companies leading in revenue reached a combined total of nearly 10 billion dollars annually and are among the 25 highest-grossing companies in the country.  Telecom, Telefónica and Claro hold the top positions.[i]  The sector benefits from substantial demand for high-speed internet, largely driven by the growing need for mobile data and broadband connectivity across the country.  Within the telecommunications industry, mobile communications hold the largest market share, representing 44% of total revenue, followed by subscription TV services at 22%, fixed internet at 18%, fixed-line telephony at 10% and audiovisual communication services at 6%.

Argentina’s telecommunications market is dominated by a few large national and multinational companies, alongside smaller regional providers.  Among the primary players are Telecom Argentina (Grupo Clarín), a comprehensive provider offering fixed-line, mobile, internet and pay-TV services; Claro (América Móvil), which holds extensive market coverage across mobile and internet sectors; and Movistar (Telefónica Group), known for its wide-reaching infrastructure and competitive services in both mobile and internet.  These leading companies generate a substantial portion of the sector’s revenue, ranking among the top 25 highest-grossing companies in the country.  They cater to a diverse range of clients, including private businesses, government entities and households.

In the audiovisual media distribution sector, annual revenue is approximately 1.5 billion dollars, primarily from television broadcasting, streaming platforms and content production.  Major players in this sector include Grupo Clarín, a dominant force in free-to-air television through Canal 13 and in cable via: Cablevisión Flow; Telefónica Group (Movistar TV), which is active in both pay-TV and streaming; and DirecTV, a significant provider of satellite television services with extensive reach across the region.

Both the telecommunications and audiovisual media sectors in Argentina are regulated by ENACOM (National Communications Entity).  ENACOM is responsible for overseeing compliance, supporting infrastructure development, and enforcing industry standards to ensure sustained growth and high-quality services across these essential sectors.

1.2        List the most important legislation that applies to the: (a) telecoms, including internet; and (b) audio-visual media distribution sectors in your jurisdiction, and list any other significant legislation on the horizon such as the regulation of online harms, regulation of social media or artificial intelligence (but only so far as they will likely impact such industries).

The provision of Information and Communications Technologies (ICT) Services in Argentina is primarily governed by Argentina Digital Law No. 27,078 (ADL).  This law establishes the regulatory framework for ICT services, aiming to promote universal access, fair competition and the integration of digital technologies across Argentina.  The ADL sets forth definitions, general rules and market standards for a broad spectrum of services, including telecommunications, internet services and audiovisual communication.

On the other hand, the provision of broadcasting services in Argentina is mainly governed by Audiovisual Communication Services Law No. 26,522 (ACS Law).  The ACS Law establishes that audiovisual communications services are an activity of public interest and includes a set of definitions, general rules and market standards.  It also covers rights and obligations for audiovisual communications services providers, regulations for advertising agencies, content producers and television channels.

Additionally, a robust consumer protection regime in the audiovisual and telecommunications sectors is vital for ensuring a fair and transparent market, where consumers are treated equitably.  In such regard, the Civil and Commercial Code governs civil and commercial relationships in Argentina, including contracts, obligations and consumer rights, thus impacting the telecoms and audiovisual sectors significantly.  Also, Consumer Protection Law No. 24,240 (mainly revised by Law No. 26,361, among others) establishes the rights of consumers and regulates market practices to ensure fairness, transparency and safety in transactions. 

Moreover, there is additional significant legislation to consider in this matter, in particular with regards to the protection of copyrights (which are specifically protected under the Intellectual Property Law No. 11,723) and of personal data, including those applicable to the use of AI tools, which encompass, among others, the Data Protection Law No. 25,326 (DPL) and its Regulatory Decree No. 1558/2001, as well as complementary regulations issued by the Argentine Agency of Access to Public Information (DPA), such as the DPA’s Resolution 4/2019 and the DPA’s “Guide for Public and Private Entities on Transparency and Protection of Personal Data for Responsible Artificial Intelligence”.

Finally, Antitrust Law No. 27,442 governs anti-competitive practices in Argentina and affects the telecoms and media sectors, especially regarding mergers, acquisitions and market power.

1.3        List the government ministries, regulators, other agencies and major industry self-regulatory bodies that have a role in the regulation of the: (a) telecoms, including internet; (b) audio-visual media distribution sectors; (c) social media platforms; and (d) artificial intelligence in your jurisdiction.

ENACOM was created by Decree No. 267/2015, as the control authority in charge of the telecommunications and audiovisual communications industry.

Another key authority is the Secretariat of Innovation, Science and Technology, which holds responsibilities related to the development of public policies and the creation of regulatory frameworks for strategic technological developments for the National Public Sector, such as artificial intelligence (AI), blockchain technology, Internet of Things, data governance, internet governance, immersive technologies and the adoption of open-source software solutions.  These initiatives are undertaken in coordination with relevant national and international bodies.

The relevant authorities from a consumer protection standpoint are the Secretariat of Industry and Commerce and the National Directorate of Consumer Protection, and each of the 23 Argentine provinces has its own consumer protection agencies that regulate the matter at the local level.

Lastly, the DPA, the Agency of Access to Public Information, oversees data protection matters in Argentina.

1.4        In relation to the: (a) telecoms, including internet; and (b) audio-visual media distribution sectors: (i) have they been liberalised?; and (ii) are they open to foreign investment, including in relation to the supply of telecoms equipment?  Are there any upper limits?

Both telecoms and audiovisual media distribution sectors in Argentina have experienced reforms promoting competition and private investment.  Regulatory oversight remains significant, although recent changes reflect a shift towards greater market flexibility.

The telecoms and audiovisual media distribution sectors are open for foreign investment under certain pre-requisites.

As regards telecoms, there are no restrictions on foreign investment other than those established for providers of internet access services by the Cultural Media Law (Law No. 25,750).  Notwithstanding the foregoing, it is worth noting that precedents of ENACOM on the application and enforceability of the Cultural Media Law are very limited.  According to available public information, the only known cases in which this law has been applied are those related to companies operating open radio and TV stations, which also qualify as “communications media companies”.  Although the law is currently in full force and effect, we are not aware that ENACOM has applied this restriction to companies providing internet access.  In any event, in case of application of this law (which is unlikely to happen), the foreign investment cap may be increased by virtue of reciprocity conditions.  There is a legislative proposal to repeal the Cultural Media Law.

Furthermore, foreign companies must at least be registered as a local branch with the local Public Registry of Commerce to apply for an ICT Licence.

The Cultural Media Law establishes certain restrictions on the participation of foreign investors in communications media companies to 30% of the entity’s voting capital.  The Media Ownership Law applies to: (i) newspapers, magazines and publishing companies in general; (ii) broadcasting services under the ACS Law (open radio and TV stations) and providers of Direct-To-Home services; (iii) producers of audiovisual and digital content; (iv) internet access providers; and (v) street advertisement companies.

The ACS Law, in line with the Cultural Media Law, also establishes restrictions on the relationship of individual and company licences with foreign companies.  These include the following:

  1. They must not have a legal corporate tie or be directly or indirectly controlled by a foreign audiovisual communications company.  Non-profit companies, their directors and counsellors cannot have direct or indirect associations with foreign audiovisual communications companies or domestic or foreign telecommunications companies in the private commercial sector.  The entity must prove that its source of funding is not directly or indirectly associated with these foreign companies.
  2. They must not be affiliates or subsidiaries of foreign companies nor may they perform acts or enter into contracts that allow a dominant position of foreign capital in the management of the licensee.
  3. Foreign equity participation of up to 30% of the share capital with voting rights is permitted, provided that such participation does not result in direct or indirect control of the company.

The limitations set out in the above points (i) and (ii) will not be considered when international treaties to which Argentina is a party establish effective reciprocity in the activity of audiovisual communications services.  The 30% foreign participation cap may be increased by virtue of the reciprocity conditions agreed on between Argentina and the foreign country where the foreign investor is based.  Reciprocity is based on the rights that the law of the country where the investor is based permits an Argentine investor to participate in broadcasting companies.

2. Telecoms

2.1        Is your jurisdiction a member of the World Trade Organization?  Has your jurisdiction made commitments under the GATS regarding telecommunications and has your jurisdiction adopted and implemented the telecoms reference paper?

Argentina has been a member of the World Trade Organization (WTO) since January 1, 1995.  In this context, Argentina has adopted the Telecoms Reference Paper of the WTO.  This document sets forth principles to ensure fair competition, including interconnection guarantees, transparent licensing procedures and the establishment of a regulatory body to prevent anti-competitive practices.  By implementing these principles, Argentina has committed to a framework that supports fair competition, non-discriminatory network access and the enforcement of regulatory standards in its telecommunications sector.

2.2        How is the provision of telecoms (or electronic communications) networks and services regulated?

Please refer to question 1.2.

2.3        Are decisions of the national regulatory authority able to be appealed?  If so, to which court or body, and on what basis?

Yes, in Argentina, decisions made by a national regulatory authority, such as ENACOM, are subject to appeal.  The right to appeal regulatory decisions is established within the administrative legal framework.

Appeals can be made to the following bodies or courts: for administrative appeals, decisions may be challenged through an administrative appeal before the same regulatory body that issued the decision or, depending on its internal structure, before the higher authority; and for judicial appeals, if the administrative appeal is unsuccessful or if the decision affects constitutional rights or is deemed arbitrary, the case may be escalated to the federal courts.

2.4        What types of general and individual authorisations are used in your jurisdiction?  Please highlight those telecom-based authorisations needed for the installation and/or maintenance of infrastructure, and the main requirements for such authorisations.

In accordance with the ADL and the Licensing Rules for ICT Services, approved by Resolution No. 697-E/2017 of the Ministry of Modernization (Licensing Rules), only one class of country-wide licence for the rendering of ICT services is established: the ICT Licence.

The ICT Licence authorises the provision of any ICT service to the public – fixed or mobile, wired or wireless, and national or international – with or without proprietary infrastructure.  ICT services may only be provided once an ICT Licence has been granted and are limited to the specific registered services under that licence.  Obtaining an ICT Licence does not mean that ENACOM guarantees the availability of necessary resources (e.g., frequency channels) to support the requested service.

Companies that solely rent their active and passive telecommunications infrastructure and do not provide other telecommunications services qualify as an Independent Operator of Passive Infrastructure (IOPI).  IOPIs are not required to apply for a licence.  However, IOPIs must: (i) notify ENACOM upon commencing rental of their infrastructure; and (ii) register the rented infrastructure in a special registry. 

2.5        In relation to individual authorisations, please identify their subject matter, duration and ability to be transferred or traded.  Are there restrictions on the change of control of the licensee?

As mentioned, an ICT Licence is required for the provision of any ICT services, and services may only be rendered as specified in the registered licence.

ICT Licences may be transferred, subject to ENACOM’s approval of the transaction.  ENACOM approval is also required for any transaction that involves a direct or indirect change of control of the licensee.  In both cases, the request for ENACOM’s approval must be submitted within 30 days of closing of the relevant transaction.  Transferring an ICT Licence or undergoing a change of control without ENACOM’s approval may result in the revocation of the ICT Licence.  If the ICT Licence is revoked, the licensee or any of its affiliates will not be able to: (i) continue providing an ICT service; nor (ii) obtain a new ICT Licence within one year following the revocation.

2.6        Are there any particular licences or other requirements (e.g. in relation to emergency services) in relation to VoIP services?

There are no specific regulations for VoIP services; general telecommunications regulations apply.  Telecommunications providers are permitted to freely determine the architecture of their ICT systems.  However, given emerging technologies, computer-based communications without access to the Public Switched Telephone Network may require further regulatory assessment to determine if a telecommunications licence is necessary.

2.7        Are there specific legal or administrative provisions dealing with access and/or securing or enforcing rights to public and private land in order to install telecommunications infrastructure?

Yes, in Argentina, there are specific legal and administrative provisions that deal with the installation of telecommunications infrastructure, particularly regarding access to public and private land.  As Argentina is a federal country, each province and municipality has its own regulations on tower siting/rights-of-way.

In general, and based on an overview of local regulatory frameworks, the following permits/authorisations may be required: (i) a construction permit; (ii) tower authorisation; (iii) environmental assessments; and (iv) a concession or permit to use public property.  When infrastructure is planned for private property, contractual rights (e.g. lease) or in rem rights (e.g. usufruct or surface right, easement) should be secured to ensure land use.

Notwithstanding the above, the Argentine Supreme Court has ruled that regulation must not interfere with the necessary infrastructure deployment for the provision of the intended ICT service, which is governed by federal rules.

2.8        Looking at fixed, mobile and other services, are charges for interconnection (e.g. switched services) and/or network access (e.g. wholesale leased lines) subject to price or cost regulation and, if so, how?

The former Ministry of Modernization issued the General Interconnection and Access Regulation under Resolution No. 286/2018, setting technical standards, economic conditions, and rules for Interconnection and Access Agreements between ICT service providers.  This regulation promotes seamless communication between users of different networks in a technologically convergent environment.

Key principles include:

  • Cost-based pricing: Interconnection charges must be transparent, cost-oriented and disaggregated, so operators pay only for the network components needed to deliver their services.
  • Non-discrimination: Rates for interconnection must be consistent and fair for both the operator’s internal and third-party users.
  • Reference offer: Operators with significant market power must provide a reference offer outlining conditions for interconnection, subject to ENACOM’s review.

In essence, Argentina’s regulatory framework seeks to ensure universal access, fair pricing, high standards and consumer protection in electronic communications.

2.9        Describe the regulation applicable to high-speed broadband networks.  On what terms are passive infrastructure (ducts and poles), copper networks, cable TV and/or fibre networks required to be made available?  Are there any incentives or ‘regulatory holidays’?

In Argentina, the regulation of high-speed broadband networks is based on the ADL and Resolution 105/2020.  These regulations promote the sharing of passive infrastructure (such as ducts, poles and shelters) and establish rules for accessing copper, fibre and cable TV networks.  The regulation mandates that ICT service licensees must allow other operators to use passive infrastructure under objective, transparent and non-discriminatory terms.  This includes access to ducts and poles to reduce costs and avoid unnecessary duplication of infrastructure.

According to Decree No. 1340/2016, a differentiated 15-year protection period is established for last-mile fixed Next-Generation Network broadbands deployed by ICT service licensees in Argentina.  This period exempts these networks from open-access broadband and infrastructure rules.

2.10      Is the provision of electronic communications services to consumers subject to any special rules (such as universal service) and if so, in what principal respects?

Yes, electronic communications services in Argentina are regulated under the Universal Service framework, established by the ADL and ENACOM’s resolutions.  This framework ensures that all Argentinians have access to affordable, quality ICT, regardless of location, social or economic conditions.

Under Universal Service, telecommunications providers must contribute 1% of their total service revenue (excluding taxes) to a fiduciary fund managed by ENACOM.  This fund finances infrastructure projects in underserved or rural areas, prioritising initiatives that bridge the digital divide and support public institutions.

2.11      How are telephone numbers and network identifying codes allocated and by whom?  Please briefly note any specific rules for telephone numbers, dynamic calling and number portability.

In Argentina, telephone numbers and network identifying codes are allocated by ENACOM under the National Fundamental Numbering Plan, established by Resolution No. 46/97 from the former Secretariat of Communications.  ENACOM oversees the efficient and non-discriminatory allocation of these resources.  Telecommunication service providers may request both geographic and non-geographic numbers, subject to regulatory oversight.  Both fixed and mobile number portability are available in Argentina.

2.12      Are there any special rules that govern the use of telephone numbers?

The National Fundamental Numbering Plan mandates a uniform length of 10 digits for all national numbers.  It differentiates between geographic numbers (linked to a specific location) and non-geographic numbers (used for special services like toll-free 800 numbers).  Certain digits, such as 0 and 1, are reserved at the beginning of numbers for access prefixes and special services.

2.13      Are there any special rules relating to dynamic calling line-identification presentation?

While Resolution No. 46/97 of the former Secretariat of Communications does not specifically address dynamic calling line identification, all numbering and call identification must adhere to established criteria to ensure services are appropriately identified within the numbering system.

2.14      Are there any obligations requiring number portability?

Both fixed and mobile number portability are available in Argentina.

3. Radio Spectrum

3.1        What authority regulates spectrum use?

The authority in charge of regulating spectrum use is ENACOM.

3.2        How is the use of radio spectrum authorised in your jurisdiction?  What procedures are used to allocate spectrum between candidates – i.e. spectrum auctions, comparative ‘beauty parades’, etc.?

Under the General Regulation for the Administration, Management and Control of the Radio Spectrum, frequencies for radio communication services can be assigned through: (a) direct allocation to national or state entities; (b) tenders or auctions; and (c) on-demand allocations based on specific requests.

Provision of ICT services requires both an ICT Licence and a technical frequency authorisation, submitted to ENACOM with a complete telecommunications project, including studies on interference, site locations and technical specifications.

Argentina also regulates permits for the experimental and temporary use of frequency bands (excluding satellite) for scientific research, demonstrations, equipment tests, events and other purposes within designated geographic areas.

3.3        Can the use of spectrum be made licence-exempt?  If so, under what conditions?  Are there penalties for the unauthorised use of spectrum?  If so, what are they?

Yes, spectrum use in Argentina can be licence-exempt under specific conditions outlined in the General Regulation for the Administration, Management and Control of Radio Spectrum.  This regulation permits licence-exempt use within designated free or collective frequency bands, typically for low-power devices like Wi-Fi equipment, which must comply with emission standards and avoid harmful interference.

Resolution No. 581/2018 of the Ministry of Modernization, along with ENACOM Resolution No. 4653/2019, allows certain frequency bands to be used by users or service providers without previous authorisation.  If ICT services are provided in these bands, an ICT Licence and registration of the corresponding ICT services are still required.

Unauthorised spectrum use is subject to penalties.  Radio stations operating without authorisation are deemed illegal and may face fines, equipment confiscation or service suspension.  ENACOM has the authority to enforce these penalties to maintain orderly spectrum use.

3.4        Are spectrum licences able to be assigned, traded or sub-licensed and, if so, on what conditions?  What happens if there is a change of control?

In Argentina, spectrum licences can be assigned, traded or sub-licensed, but this process is tightly regulated under the General Regulation for the Administration, Management and Control of Radio Spectrum and the ADL.

Spectrum licence holders may transfer or assign their licences to another party, but only with prior authorisation from ENACOM.  Transfers are permitted provided the assignee meets regulatory requirements and can continue the services authorised under the licence.

These regulations ensure that spectrum resources are used effectively and that control over telecommunications services remains with approved entities, safeguarding market stability and service quality.

4. Cyber-security, Interception, Encryption and Data Retention

4.1        Describe the legal framework for cybersecurity.  Are there any specific requirements in relation to telecoms operators or cloud providers?

Argentina does not have a specific law governing cybersecurity matters.  However, there are some rules aimed at protecting information security and communications technology systems, which include, among others: Law No. 26,388 on Cyber Crimes; Resolution No. 580/2011 of the Chief of Staff; Decree No. 577/2017, as amended by Decree No. 480/2019; Resolution No. 1523/2019 of the Secretariat of Modernization; Resolution No. 829/2019 of the Secretariat of Modernization; Administrative Decision No. 641/2021; Resolution No. 1/2021 of the Cybersecurity National Office; Resolution No. 7/2021 of the Cybersecurity National Office; Resolution No. 8/2021 of the Cybersecurity National Office; Resolution No. 1/2022 of the Cybersecurity National Office; Resolution No. 3/2023 of the Undersecretariat for Information Technologies; Resolution No. 44/2023 of the Public Innovation Secretariat; Resolution No. 15/2024 of the Secretary of Innovation, Science and Technology; Resolution 75/2022; Decree No. 50/2019; Law No. 27,411; Confidentiality Law No. 24,766; the DPL and its Regulatory Decree No. 1558/2001; DPA’s Resolution No. 47/2018; and DPA’s Resolution No. 332/2020.

Among the most relevant resolutions are: (i) Administrative Decision No. 641/2021, which requires the organisations to develop an Information Security Policy compatible with their primary responsibilities and competencies; (ii) Decree No. 577/2017, amended by 480/2019, which creates the Cybersecurity Committee; (iii) Resolution No. 829/2019 of the Secretariat of Modernization, which approves the Argentine Cybersecurity Strategy, which establishes that the Cybersecurity Committee should promote public and private efforts to build capacities for the detection, protection and response to threats and attacks, based on the resources and responsibilities of each organisation; (iv) Resolution No. 75/2022, which approved a federal plan for the prevention of technological crime and cybercrime 2021–2024; (v) Decree No. 50/2019, which determines the primary responsibility for cybersecurity and protection of critical information and communications infrastructure; and (vi) Law No. 27,411, which approves the Council of Europe Convention on Cybercrime ratified by Argentina.

In addition, there are a number of consulting documents issued by and for public agencies intended to serve as recommendations and guidelines based on the provision of cloud services by the Argentine Company of Satellite Solutions (ARSAT), among which the adequacy and compliance with ISO 9001, ISO 27001, 27002 and ISO 27017, 27018 standards are recommended, as well as data protection standards and laws.

4.2        Describe the legal framework (including listing relevant legislation) that governs the ability of the state (police, security services, etc.) to obtain access to private communications.

The legal framework in Argentina regulating state access to private communications is compounded by various laws.

  • The Argentine Constitution (Art. 18) protects the right of private and secret communication.  Only a law can determine in which cases and under what justifications a search and seizure may proceed.  Therefore, interventions are only permissible under strict conditions and require judicial authorisation.  Furthermore, Art. 19 of the Argentine Constitution protects the right of privacy in general.
  • The ADL governs the protection of privacy in electronic communications and establishes that interception, as well as subsequent recording and analysis, may only proceed upon the request of a competent judge.  This provision is aligned with section 10 of the DPL, which establishes that the duty of confidentiality applicable to the processing of personal data may only be relieved by judicial decision or when there are well-founded reasons relating to public security, national defence or public health.
  • The National Code of Criminal Procedure regulates explicitly the interception of telephone communications in its Art. 236, requiring in all cases a grounded court order. 
  • In the Argentine Criminal Code, Arts 153 to 157 bis regulate crimes regarding the violation of secrets and privacy, establishing criminal definitions that punish unauthorised interception of private communications.

4.3        Summarise the rules that require market participants to maintain call interception (wire-tap) capabilities.  Does this cover: (i) traditional telephone calls; (ii) VoIP calls; (iii) emails; and (iv) any other forms of communications?

According to Law No. 25,873, all telecommunications service providers must have the necessary human and technological resources for the capture and derivation of the communications they transmit, for their remote observation at the request of the Judiciary or the Public Prosecutor’s Office in accordance with the legislation in force.

Telecommunications service providers shall bear the costs derived from such obligation and shall immediately comply with it at all times and on all days of the year.

Telecommunication service providers shall register and systematise the affiliation and domiciliary data of their users and customers and the traffic records of communications made by them for consultation free of charge by the Judiciary or the Public Prosecutor’s Office.  The information referred to herein shall be kept by telecommunications service providers for a term of 10 years.

In accordance with this, telecommunications carriers and phone companies, as well as e-mail service providers or any person or company related to telecommunications activities, may be requested to allow authorities to access their records and their customers’ communications.

Although some of the laws that rule lawful access only mention phone interception, in accordance with a reasonable interpretation of said rules, lawful access can be requested or performed on any other sort of communication (postal, telegraph, fax or any other system used to send objects and/or to transmit images, voices or data packages, as well as any type of information, files and/or private documents).

4.4        Describe the rules governing the use of encryption and the circumstances when encryption keys need to be provided to the state.

Although this is not explicitly regulated in the existing legislation, it is reasonable to interpret that data should be provided to authorities in a manner that is useful for the relevant investigation.

4.5        What data are telecoms or internet infrastructure operators obliged to retain and for how long?

Please refer to question 4.3.

From a personal data protection perspective, in addition, there are currently no obligations for data retention under the DPL that apply specifically to telecommunications or internet infrastructure operators, nor is there a specified retention period established therein.  In this sense, the DPL establishes that personal data must be destroyed, even without the express request of the data subject, when they are no longer necessary or relevant for the purposes for which they were collected.  Additionally, the DPL authorises data controllers to retain personal data based on specific applicable rules (such as labour, corporate or tax law), or during the terms agreed between the data controller and the data subjects.

5. Distribution of Audio-Visual Media

5.1        How is the distribution of audio-visual media regulated in your jurisdiction?

The distribution of audiovisual media in Argentina is mainly governed by the ACS Law.  The ACS Law established that audiovisual communications services are an activity of public interest and includes a set of definitions, general rules and market standards.  It also covers rights and obligations for audiovisual communications services providers, regulations for advertising agencies, content producers and television channels.

In addition, under Law No. 24,240 (Consumer Protection Law) and the Civil and Commercial Code, consumers are entitled to clear information, fair treatment, high-quality services and access to mechanisms for resolving disputes.  It should be considered that any provision made in the advertisement is considered part of the binding offer to consumers.  Regulation such as Resolution No. 12/2024 from the Secretariat of Industry and Commerce foresees rigorous formal requirements at the time of displaying advertising on television, graphic and digital media.  The consumer protection regime establishes rules around transparency, hidden fees and advertising, and companies must ensure all information is clear and accessible.

5.2        Is content regulation (including advertising, as well as editorial) different for content broadcast via traditional distribution platforms as opposed to content delivered over the internet or other platforms?  Please describe the main differences.

Yes, in Argentina, traditional broadcasting platforms regulated by the ACS Law are subject to content standards, especially regarding violence and offensive language during family-friendly hours.  Advertising on these platforms has time limits, content restrictions and industry-specific rules (e.g. regarding alcohol and tobacco).

Local content quotas require private audio broadcasters to air at least 70% domestically produced content, with 30% dedicated to domestic music and 50% to self-produced content, including news.  Open television networks must broadcast a minimum of 60% domestic productions.

In contrast, OTT content delivered over the internet is not subject to ACS Law and faces fewer restrictions, allowing greater flexibility in language, themes and advertising.  While OTT advertising has no specific time limits or separation standards, it must comply with general consumer protection laws to avoid misleading information.  Additionally, ACS Law and non-binding influencer guidelines serve as references for adopting good practices in content and advertising.

In this regard, the National Directorate of Consumer Protection also regulates advertising across all media, both on the internet and in traditional broadcasts.  Under recent Resolution No. 12/2024 from the Secretariat of Industry and Commerce, platforms must now ensure greater transparency and clarity in sponsored content disclosures, with specific requirements for digital platforms to make advertising easily identifiable.  These new obligations have reduced the flexibility previously enjoyed by online platforms.

5.3        Describe the different types of licences for the distribution of audio-visual media and their key obligations.

An individual broadcasting licence is required under the ACS Law for the distribution of audiovisual media, radio and open TV (ACS Licence). Subscription television services, however, are governed by the ADL under the ICT services framework.  Subscription broadcasting includes services via satellite, physical link and radio electric link.

To obtain an ACS Licence, individuals must be Argentine citizens or naturalised citizens with at least five years of residence, demonstrate the origin of the investment funds, and have no civil or criminal disqualifications.  For legal entities, restrictions on foreign investment apply.

ACS Licences requiring non-satellite radio spectrum are awarded through an open, permanent bidding process.  Licences for providers serving areas larger than 50km with over 500,000 inhabitants are issued by the Argentine Executive, while ENACOM awards licences for smaller coverage areas via bidding.

ACS Licences are valid for 10 years, extendable by five years upon request to ENACOM, with further 10-year renewals possible.  However, a bidding process may be held to grant new licences.

5.4        Are licences assignable?  If not, what rules apply?  Are there restrictions on change of control of the licensee?

Yes, licences granted under the ACS Law may be assigned.  However, ENACOM’s post-closing approval is required for any transaction involving: (i) the transfer of an audiovisual communications licence; or (ii) a direct or indirect transfer of shares in an audiovisual communications service company (Media Transaction).

The request to ENACOM for approval must be submitted within 30 days of closing the Media Transaction, and the purchaser must provide evidence to ENACOM of the fulfilment of all regulatory requirements for becoming the owner of such licence.  The implementation of a Media Transaction without ENACOM’s approval may result in revocation of the licence.

6. Internet Infrastructure

6.1        How have the courts interpreted and applied any defences (e.g. ‘mere conduit’ or ‘common carrier’) available to protect telecommunications operators and/or internet service providers from liability for content carried over their networks?

In Argentina, there is no specific legislation addressing the liability of intermediaries, such as internet service providers (ISPs) or search engines.  Courts rely on the general principles of civil liability established in the Civil and Commercial Code, distinguishing between objective and subjective liability.  Objective liability applies when a party is responsible due to ownership or control of a risk-creating asset, while subjective liability is based on negligence or intent.

The Argentine Supreme Court of Justice (CSJN) has ruled in cases involving Google and Yahoo, favouring subjective liability for search engines.  In that regard, courts have concluded that ISPs and search engines do not have a general obligation to monitor third-party created content.  Moreover, the Supreme Court has also concluded that ISPs/search engines could be held liable if they acquire actual knowledge that certain content is unlawful (e.g. violating third-party rights) and fail to promptly and diligently take action to remove the content after.  In the case of content manifestly unlawful, out-of-court notifications will suffice.  In all other cases, a court notification is required.  Moreover, the Supreme Court has also concluded that ISPs/search engines could have actual knowledge if they have an active role, which would allow them to effectively become aware of the infringement.

Please note that in Argentina, court decisions only have a moral binding effect on lower courts, as the stare decisis principle does not apply.

6.2        Are telecommunications operators and/or internet service providers under any obligations (i.e. to provide information, inform customers, disconnect customers) to assist content owners whose rights may be infringed by means of file-sharing or other activities?

Telecommunications operators and ISPs in Argentina have no specific legal obligations to assist content owners in protecting their rights from infringements, such as file sharing, other than obligations arising from general principles of civil and criminal law.  Please also refer to question 6.1.

6.3        Briefly summarise any ‘net neutrality’ requirements in your jurisdiction. 

According to the ADL, the principle of net neutrality guarantees each user the right to access, use, send, receive or offer any content, application, service or protocol over the internet without restriction, discrimination, distinction, blocking, interference, hindrance or degradation.

ICT service providers may not block, interfere with, discriminate against, hinder, degrade or restrict the use, sending, receiving, offering or accessing of any content, application, service or protocol except by court order or at the express request of the user.  Additionally, providers may not set internet access prices based on the content, services, protocols or applications used or offered under their contracts.

6.4        Are telecommunications operators and/or internet service providers under any obligations to block access to certain sites or content?  Are consumer VPN services regulated or blocked?

Yes, telecommunication operators and/or ISPs may be required to block certain websites or content, particularly under specific judicial orders.  ISPs must block access to websites containing illegal content, such as those infringing copyright or hosting explicit illegal material, when ordered by a court.  This includes sites identified by regulatory authorities or those involved in criminal activities.

Regarding consumer VPN services, there are currently no specific regulations or blockings in place.

6.5        Is there any regulation applicable to companies that act as ‘intermediaries’ or ‘platforms’ in their role of connecting consumers with goods, services or content, or are there any proposals for such regulation?  Briefly include any proposals or legislation regulating social media platforms in relation to online content or safety.

In Argentina, there is no specific regulation that comprehensively defines the liability of companies acting as intermediaries or platforms connecting consumers with goods, services or content.  However, Argentine caselaw has extensively addressed this issue through various cases, establishing criteria to determine the responsibility of these platforms.

One prominent approach in the courts involves analysing the nature of the intermediary’s role: whether the platform maintains an active or passive position in the transaction or content presentation.  Case law has determined that when the intermediary takes a passive role (without influencing or modifying third-party content), an exemption from liability may apply.  Conversely, if the intermediary plays an active role, such as optimising product displays or promoting offers, it assumes a higher degree of responsibility.

The courts have adopted a subjective liability model, whereby the intermediary is liable only if, after becoming aware of illegal or infringing content, it fails to take the necessary steps to remove it.  Notable cases include Kosten v. Mercado Libre SRL and Nike International Ltd. v. Compañía de Medios Digitales, in which the intermediary’s liability was analysed based on its level of control and knowledge over transactions.

Regarding social media platforms, Argentina currently lacks specific legislation regulating them in terms of content or online safety.  However, there is an ongoing debate about the liability of these platforms and proposals to legislate intermediary liability and establish user protection measures to address online safety and offensive content issues.

7. Industry Changes

7.1        What do you think is the biggest change that players in the industry are facing?  How are regulators, providers and other relevant institutions preparing for this change?

The biggest imminent change that players in the industry are facing is the continuous evolution of AI.  This technology is advancing at an unprecedented pace, and the industry players must keep up to avoid the risks associated with falling behind.  To mitigate these risks, it is essential to stay informed about every update and proactively anticipate their implications and consequences.

In this regard, the DPA has issued the “Guide for Public and Private Entities on Transparency and Protection of Personal Data for Responsible Artificial Intelligence”, which aims to provide recommendations that help both public and private entities to incorporate the principles of transparency and personal data protection in technological development projects that implement AI systems, in order to guarantee the effective exercise of citizens’ rights.  In order to prevent unwanted risks, this guide helps entities to take action and specific measures accordingly.

In line with the above, the telecommunications industry is also undergoing a major transformation through the integration of AI, which is optimising processes and enhancing service delivery.  AI enables automation in network management, bandwidth optimisation and predictive analytics for improved customer service, helping providers ensure stable, high-quality connections.  To support AI-driven advancements, investment in data centres is essential, as they provide the computing power necessary for AI processing and data storage.  There are several legislative proposals to regulate the development of AI.

Satellite facilities also play a critical role, especially in connecting remote regions.  In Argentina, low-Earth orbit (LEO) satellites are expanding high-speed, low-latency internet access to underserved areas, helping reduce the digital divide.  Regulators, including ENACOM, are establishing data privacy standards, supporting local data centre development, and creating frameworks for LEO satellite operations and spectrum allocation.  These efforts aim to facilitate competition in satellite internet services while ensuring data protection and user trust in AI-powered services.

The deployment of 5G in Argentina presents a significant challenge for the telecoms industry, requiring substantial investment in infrastructure, spectrum allocation and regulatory support.  The rollout demands network upgrades, increased data centre capacity and extensive fibre optics to enable high-speed, low-latency connectivity across the country.  Additionally, regulatory frameworks must adapt to support efficient spectrum use and foster competitive, sustainable 5G services for consumers and businesses.

An emerging factor in this landscape is the role of influencers, who have become significant players in content creation and distribution.  Influencers leverage social media to engage audiences directly, often blurring the lines between advertising and organic content.  While this offers opportunities for targeted marketing, it raises regulatory challenges.  Although the National Directorate of Consumer Protection has attempted to address the lack of transparency in influencer advertising, there still appears to be no clear regulatory framework in place.
